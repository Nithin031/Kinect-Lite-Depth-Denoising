Each cleaner works with one tracker and fails with the other. So the question is what each cleaner changes in the depth image, and what each tracker is sensitive to.

2. What each cleaner does to the depth

Median

Removes spikes (RMSE 187 to 55 mm). Does little to the ordinary small noise.

Paper

Smooths the ordinary noise by averaging neighbours and nearby frames. Keeps the spikes (RMSE stays at 186 mm), because a spike has no similar neighbours to average with.

3. What each tracker does with a spike

Depth-only ICP

Matches each 3D point to its nearest point in the other frame, then throws away pairs that are too far apart (10, 5, then 2 cm in our code). A spike is far from everything, so it is dropped.

RGB-D odometry

Compares every pixel directly. It has no cut-off, so a spike pulls the result.

Try it. Twenty points on a flat wall are measured with small noise. The true error is zero. Add a spike and switch the cut-off on or off.

This is a one-number toy: it estimates a single offset as the mean of the points it keeps. Real ICP solves for a full camera motion, but the effect of a cut-off on a spike is the same.

4. What still needs checking

Sections 2 and 3 explain why the paper fails with RGB-D (it keeps the spikes) and why the median works with RGB-D (it removes them). They also explain why spikes do not hurt ICP.

They do not fully explain why the median fails with ICP. What the numbers show:

  • The camera moves 6.5 mm per tracked step. The median's ICP error per step is 11.78 mm, larger than the motion. The paper's is 2.02 mm.
  • The paper has worse per-pixel depth error than the median (MAE 37.7 against 18.8 mm) and still tracks better. So depth accuracy alone does not predict ICP accuracy.

The project's explanation is that the paper gives smoother surfaces and therefore better surface normals, which ICP needs. That has not been tested directly. A quick test would be to compute the normals from each cleaner's output and compare them with the normals from the true depth.
